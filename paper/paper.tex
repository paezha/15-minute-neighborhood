  \begin{abstract}
  Fifteen-minutes neihborhoods, a form of normative chronourbanism based
  on cumulative opportunities, has aroused interest as a way to reduce
  the need for motorized travel, and increase the livability,
  convenience, and health of the public. At the core of this concept is
  a pedshed, an area defined by the walkable isochrone of the eponymous
  fifteen minutes. As the idea of fifteen-minutes neighborhoods develops
  traction in policy and planning circles, it seems timely to revisit
  the way street network design can support---or obstruct---the stated
  goal of preserving or creating walkable neighborhoods with essential
  amenities. In this paper we examine a sample (\(n=834\)) of
  fifteen-minutes pedsheds in Hamilton, a medium-sized city in Canada,
  and how their sizes relate to the attributes of the transportation
  network. The analysis reveals that network design in suburban Hamilton
  conspires against the creating of fifteen-minutes neighborhoods. Much
  of urban Hamilton, in contrast, already has the characteristics of
  fifteen-minutes neighborhoods. The research points to elements of
  network design that can help to discriminate between candidate
  neighborhoods and dead-ends, and that can provide parameters for the
  design of new developments.

\section{Data}\label{data}

Relevant data to develop this paper comprises to use 15-min pedestrian
sheds (or walksehds) classified into urban and suburban types in
Hamilton.

15-minute walksheds in the city of Hamilton were obtained by extracting
relevant structural attributes of the street network within 15-minute
pedestrian sheds. These sheds were calculated for each shed as polygon
representations of origin-destination walking time matrices, with a
maximum travel time of 15 minutes. Walkshed calculations were generated
using the travel\_time\_matrix function for walking from the \{r5r\} R
package. This process involved defining the street network, as well as
the origins and destinations of walking trips. The street network data
for these calculations was sourced from OpenStreetMap
\citet{OpenStreetMap2023}.\\

The origins we established are proxies for the centroids of
neighborhoods in the urban and peri-urban areas of the city of Hamilton.
Specifically, these are the centroids of Hamilton's Dissemination Areas
(DA). A DA is composed of one or more neighboring dissemination blocks
and represents the smallest standard geographic area for which all
census data are disseminated in Canada. Data on urban and sub-urban
boundaries, DAs,and population data in 2016 were retrieved from
\citet{HamiltonData2023}.

Destinations were established as the centroids of geohashed hexagons
within the region of interest, where Hamilton's amenities are located,
assuming that presence of amenities is a proxy to provision of
opportunities. These destinations were chosen to avoid calculating
travel times to each individual amenity. Instead, we used the geohashed
polygons as destinations, identifying the amenities within each polygon
and assuming that the centroid of each hexagon represents the location
of the amenities within it. Specifically, we retrieved geohashing data
for Hamilton using the H3 system \citet{h3Package} and generated child
hexagons at resolution 13, with an average area of 43.87 \(m^2\).
Amenities location and their typology were downloaded from
\citet{OpenStreetMap2023}.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

The proposed method involves studying 15-minute walking neighborhoods in
two stages. In the first stage, we assess the accessibility of
Hamilton's walksheds from a normative perspective, focusing on the
availability of opportunities. In the second stage, we analyze the
topology of urban and suburban walksheds based on various network
structure attributes. Finally, we examine whether there are differences
in transportation network topology between urban and suburban areas, and
analyze how these differences affect access to opportunities within
walksheds.

\subsection{Calculation of accessibility
levels}\label{calculation-of-accessibility-levels}

The indicator we use assesses levels of accessibility from a normative
perspective, focusing on reachable opportunities as estimates of the
potential for spatial interactions \citet{Hansen1959}. The advantage of
using this form of accessibility analysis in this study is that it has
been shown to be a valuable method for exploring the relationship
between transportation infrastructure and urban structure
\citet{Handy1997}.

In this study, we calculate cumulative accessibility scores by summing
the number of amenities within urban and suburban walksheds, categorized
into the four types provided by the data source: financial, sustenance,
healthcare, and library. Additionally, we extract population data of
each walkshed. Summary statistics of population are also included in the
analysis.

Additionally, the accessibility assessment is completed by obtaining
summary statistics of travel times in urban and suburban walksheds. To
study the impact of urban and suburban walksheds on walking decisions,
minimum and mean travel times to destinations are introduced into
time-decay functions for walking decisions, as developed by Milward
(2013). These are active travel impedance functions based on data from
Canada.

\subsubsection{Modelling}\label{modelling}

To model urban and suburban walksheds, we employ a classification tree
approach using the set topology attributes calculated in the previous
stage. Classification trees are machine learning techniques used for
both regression and classification, widely appreciated in various
disciplines for their ability to represent knowledge through decision
rules, making them easy to interpret and analyze \citet{Loh2011}
\citet{James2013} \citet{Paez2019Trees}. Specifically, we utilize
evolutionary trees, a variant of decision trees that are less
constrained by the limitations of greedy top-down induction algorithms
\citet{Cha2009} \citet{Kretowski2007} \citet{Grubinger2014}. Since
evolutionary trees use a genetic algorithm that introduces randomness in
the initial conditions, we run the model from the R package evtree
\citet{Grubinger2014} with 20 random seeds to identify the network
variables that consistently characterize urban and suburban walksheds.

The results of training an evolutionary classification tree are shown in
Fig. \ref{fig:evolutionary-tree}. This model addresses our first
research question (are networks different between urban and suburban
regions?). The answer to this question is unambiguously affirmative. As
seen in the figure, the tree has four terminal nodes (or leaves). The
purity of the leaves (meaning the mix of classes in a leaf) is very
high, since each leaf is composed largely of a single class (77.4\% and
80.2\% Suburban in Leaves 1 and 3 respectively, and 88.8\% and 87.9\%
Urban in Leaves 2 and 4 respectively). Thus, there are two kinds each of
urban and suburban walksheds, depending on the attributes of the network
that generated them.

Of the attributes in Table \ref{tab:network-attributes}, only two are
picked up by the classification algorithm: edge density and motifs of
size 3. The first attribute that helps to discriminate between urban and
suburban walksheds is edge density. The cutoff value of edge density is
0.00590. Recall that edge density is the number of edges in the graph
relative to the maximum possible number of edges--that is, compared to a
fully connected graph. This means that the cutoff is about 0.59\% of the
number of edges in a fully connected graph with the same number of
vertices. The range of values of this variable in our walksheds is
0.117\% and 40\% (the mean is 1.246\%). But there are marked differences
in the value of this attribute by Leaf in the classification tree, as
